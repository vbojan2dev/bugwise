Marta orders her groceries online every Sunday. She is blind and uses a <Term id="screen-reader" /> on her laptop. This week the shop released a redesigned checkout. She reaches the payment step and hears: *"button. button. edit text. edit text. button."* No labels, no hints. She guesses, gets an error she can't hear, and gives up. She orders from a competitor instead, and the shop never finds out why it lost a loyal customer.

Nothing "crashed". Every automated test passed. And yet, for Marta, the checkout was **completely broken**. Finding this kind of bug is what <Term id="accessibility-testing">accessibility testing</Term> is about, and you don't need special tools or a special job title to start.

## Why it matters

Accessibility (often shortened to **a11y**, because there are 11 letters between the "a" and the "y") means people can use a product **whatever their abilities, devices or situation**. The World Health Organization estimates that about **1.3 billion people, roughly 1 in 6**, live with a significant disability. But accessibility helps far more people than that:

| Kind | Example | What helps them |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Permanent** | Blind, low vision, deaf, one arm, tremor, dyslexia | Screen reader support, captions, keyboard access, clear language |
| **Temporary** | Broken wrist, eye surgery, lost glasses, ear infection | Keyboard access, zoom, captions |
| **Situational** | Holding a baby in one arm, bright sunlight on the screen, a noisy train, a quiet library | One-handed use, good contrast, captions, no sound needed |
| **Age-related** | Weaker eyesight, less precise fingers, slower reactions | Larger text, bigger targets, no time limits |

<Callout type="example" title="The curb cut effect">

Curb cuts (the small ramps in pavements) were built for wheelchair users. Today they're used by people with strollers, suitcases, bikes and delivery trolleys. Captions, dark mode, voice control and keyboard shortcuts all started the same way. **Design for the edges and everyone benefits.**

</Callout>

See also: [Usability Testing Basics](/fundamentals/usability-testing) for the wider question of whether *all* users can reach their goals easily.

### The legal side (briefly)

Accessibility is increasingly a **legal requirement**, not just good practice. You don't need to be a lawyer, but you should know why your team cares:

- **European Union:** the **European Accessibility Act** applies from **28 June 2025** to many consumer products and services, including e-commerce, banking, e-books, and passenger transport ticketing. Very small service companies (microenterprises) are exempt. In practice, teams meet it by following the European standard EN 301 549, which builds on WCAG AA.
- **United States:** the **ADA** (Americans with Disabilities Act) has been used in many lawsuits about inaccessible websites and apps. In 2024 the Department of Justice adopted **WCAG 2.1 AA** as the technical standard for state and local government websites and mobile apps (in 2026 it pushed the compliance deadlines back by a year). Federal agencies follow **Section 508**.
- **Elsewhere:** the UK, Canada, Australia, Israel and many other countries have their own laws, most of them pointing to WCAG as well.

<Callout type="note">
Laws, deadlines and exceptions vary by country and change over time. Your job as a tester is not to give legal advice: it's to find and clearly report the barriers. Ask your team which standard and level you're testing against.
</Callout>

## WCAG in five minutes

The <Term id="wcag">Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG)</Term> are the international standard almost every law and company refers to. They're published by the W3C. The current version is **WCAG 2.2** (October 2023). Many contracts and laws still name **2.1**, and 2.2 includes everything in 2.1 (except one obsolete parsing rule), so testing against 2.2 covers both.

### The four principles: POUR

Every WCAG rule (called a **success criterion**) belongs to one of four principles:

### Levels A, AA and AAA

Each success criterion has a level:

| Level | Meaning | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **A** | The minimum. Without it, some people can't use the page at all | Images have text alternatives, everything works with a keyboard, no keyboard traps |
| **AA** | The usual target for laws and company policies | Text contrast 4.5:1, visible focus, text can be resized to 200%, consistent navigation |
| **AAA** | The highest level, for specialized content. Not required for whole sites | Text contrast 7:1, sign language for videos, no time limits at all |

<Callout type="tip" title="AA is the target">
When someone says "the site must be accessible", they almost always mean **WCAG 2.1 or 2.2, level AA**. Level AA includes all level A criteria too. Mention the level and the criterion number in bug reports, for example **"WCAG 2.2, 1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum), AA"**.
</Callout>

WCAG 2.2 added a few criteria testers should know, such as **2.4.11 Focus Not Obscured (Minimum)** (a sticky header or cookie banner must not completely hide the focused element), **2.5.8 Target Size (Minimum)** (click/tap targets at least **24×24 CSS pixels**, with some exceptions) and **3.3.8 Accessible Authentication (Minimum)** (don't force people to solve puzzles or retype codes from memory to log in; allow password managers and paste).

## The most common failures and how to check them

Most accessibility bugs fall into a handful of patterns. For each one, here's what to look for and how a manual tester checks it.

### 1. Keyboard access and visible focus

Many people can't use a mouse: people with motor disabilities, power users, and everyone using a screen reader on a computer. <Term id="keyboard-navigation">Keyboard navigation</Term> must reach and operate **every** interactive element.

**How to check:** put the mouse away. Press <kbd>Tab</kbd> from the top of the page.

- Can you reach every link, button, field and menu? Custom controls (div "buttons", custom dropdowns, sliders) are the usual suspects.
- Can you **see** where you are at all times? A clear focus indicator is required (WCAG 2.4.7). Designers sometimes remove it with `outline: none` because it "looks ugly".
- Can you operate controls? <kbd>Enter</kbd> activates links and buttons, <kbd>Space</kbd> toggles checkboxes and presses buttons, arrow keys move inside radio groups, menus and tabs, <kbd>Esc</kbd> closes dialogs.
- Can you get **out**? Getting stuck inside a widget is a keyboard trap (2.1.2). Modals are the exception: they should keep focus inside on purpose (a <Term id="focus-trap" />) until closed. See [Modal](/components/modal).
- Is the focused element hidden behind a sticky header or cookie banner (2.4.11)?

### 2. Focus order

Focus should move in a **logical order**, usually the visual reading order: top to bottom, left to right (2.4.3). Watch out for focus jumping to the footer, skipping a field, or going to elements hidden off-screen. When a dialog opens, focus should move into it; when it closes, it should return to the button that opened it.

**How to check:** Tab through and say out loud where you expect focus to go next. Any surprise is a finding.

### 3. Color contrast

Low-contrast text is the single most common failure on the web. The <Term id="contrast-ratio">contrast ratio</Term> between text and its background must be at least:

| What | AA | AAA |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Normal text | **4.5:1** | 7:1 |
| Large text (at least 24 px regular, or about 18.7 px bold) | **3:1** | 4.5:1 |
| UI parts and meaningful graphics: input borders, icons, focus rings, chart lines | **3:1** | n/a |

Typical offenders: light grey hint or placeholder text, white text on orange or yellow buttons, text over photos, disabled-looking but active buttons. (Truly disabled controls are exempt.)

<ContrastChecker client:visible />

**How to check:** use the color picker in a contrast tool (or in DevTools: inspect the text, click the color swatch in the Styles pane, and DevTools shows the contrast ratio with AA/AAA checks). Check states too: hover, focus, error, and dark mode.

### 4. Don't rely on color alone

About **1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women** have some form of color vision deficiency. If an error is shown only by turning a border red, or a chart uses only red and green lines, some users miss it entirely (1.4.1).

**How to check:** use a greyscale or color-blindness filter. Can you still tell what's wrong, what's selected, which line is which? Errors need **text** (and ideally an icon), links in body text need an underline or other non-color cue.

### 5. Images and alt text

Every meaningful image needs a text alternative (1.1.1). Screen readers read the <Term id="alt-text" /> instead of the image.

<Compare>
<Do title="Describes the purpose">

`alt="Red running shoes, side view"` on a product photo.

`alt=""` on a purely decorative swirl, so screen readers skip it.

An icon-only button with an accessible name: `aria-label="Search"`.

</Do>
<Dont title="Missing, useless or redundant">

No alt at all: the screen reader may read the file name, like *"IMG_2041.jpg"*.

`alt="image"` or `alt="photo"`: tells the user nothing.

`alt="Picture of a button that says Buy now"`: just say what it does.

</Dont>
</Compare>

**How to check:** in DevTools, inspect images and look at the `alt` attribute, or use an extension like WAVE that shows alt text on top of the page. Then ask: if I couldn't see this image, would the alt text give me the same information?

### 6. Form labels and error messages

Forms are where accessibility bugs hurt most, because they block sign-ups, payments and bookings.

- Every field needs a **visible label** that is **programmatically connected** to it. Otherwise a screen reader just says *"edit text"*.
  <DevNote>`<label for="…">` or `aria-labelledby`.</DevNote>
- **Placeholder-only labels** fail: the <Term id="placeholder" /> disappears as soon as you type, it's usually low contrast, and users with memory difficulties lose track of what the field was for.
- Errors must be **described in text** (3.3.1), say how to fix it ("Enter a date like 31/12/2025"), be connected to the field and be announced when they appear.
  <DevNote>Connected with `aria-describedby` and `aria-invalid="true"`.</DevNote>
- Required fields must be indicated in a way that isn't color alone.

See [Text Field](/components/text-field) and [Login and Sign Up](/features/login-and-sign-up) for more form-specific checks.

### 7. Headings and structure

Screen reader users don't read pages top to bottom: they **jump between headings** to skim, just like sighted users scan. Pages need a logical heading structure (1.3.1, 2.4.6): one `h1`, then `h2` for sections, `h3` inside them, and no "fake" headings made of big bold text.

**How to check:** use a browser extension that shows the heading outline (WAVE, or the "Headings" view in a screen reader). Does the outline make sense on its own?

### 8. Link and button text

Screen reader users often pull up a list of all links. A list of *"click here, click here, read more, read more"* is useless.

<Compare>
<Do title="Makes sense on its own">

[Download the March invoice (PDF, 120 KB)](#)

</Do>
<Dont title="Needs the surrounding text">

To download your invoice, [click here](#).

</Dont>
</Compare>

### 9. Zoom 200% and reflow

People with low vision zoom in. Text must be resizable to **200%** without losing content or functionality (1.4.4), and at a width of **320 CSS pixels** (the same as 400% zoom on a 1280 px wide window) content must **reflow** into one column without horizontal scrolling (1.4.10).

**How to check:** press <kbd>Ctrl</kbd>/<kbd>Cmd</kbd> + <kbd>+</kbd> until the browser shows 200%, then 400%. Look for cut-off text, overlapping elements, buttons pushed off-screen and sticky headers that cover half the screen. On mobile, also test the system text size (iOS **Larger Text** / <Term id="dynamic-type" />, Android **Font size**).

### 10. Motion and animation

Parallax effects, zooming transitions and auto-playing carousels can cause dizziness and nausea for people with vestibular disorders. Anything that moves, blinks or scrolls automatically for more than 5 seconds needs a way to **pause, stop or hide it** (2.2.2), nothing may flash more than **3 times per second** (2.3.1), and sites should respect the user's **Reduce motion** setting.

**How to check:** turn on Reduce motion (macOS: System Settings › Accessibility › Display; iOS: Settings › Accessibility › Motion; Windows: Settings › Accessibility › Visual effects › Animation effects off) and check that big animations calm down. Look for a pause button on carousels and videos.

### 11. Captions and transcripts

Videos need **captions** for speech and important sounds (1.2.2 for pre-recorded video, 1.2.4 for live video at AA). Audio-only content like podcasts needs a **transcript**. Auto-generated captions are a start, but check that they're accurate, especially names, numbers and product terms.

**How to check:** play every video with the sound off. Can you follow it? Are captions in sync and correct?

## Common bugs

| # | Bug | What you see | Typical severity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Field without a label** | Screen reader says "edit text" with no name | High (blocks forms) |
| 2 | **Focus outline removed** | Tabbing through the page, you can't tell where you are | High |
| 3 | **Custom control not keyboard operable** | A div "checkbox", dropdown or button that Tab skips or Enter/Space ignores | Critical when it blocks a key flow |
| 4 | **Low text contrast** | Light grey hints, placeholders, white on yellow | Medium |
| 5 | **Error shown only in color** | Red border, no message; screen reader says nothing | High |
| 6 | **Icon button with no accessible name** | Screen reader says just "button" | High |
| 7 | **Missing or bad alt text** | File names read aloud, or "image" with no meaning | Medium–High |
| 8 | **Fake or skipped headings** | Big bold text that isn't a heading; h1 → h4 jumps | Medium |
| 9 | **Broken layout at 200% zoom** | Text cut off, overlapping, content needs horizontal scrolling | Medium–High |
| 10 | **Focus lost after a dialog closes** | Focus jumps back to the top of the page | Medium |
| 11 | **Auto-playing motion with no pause** | Carousel or background video that never stops | Medium |
| 12 | **Videos without captions** | Deaf users can't follow the content | High |

<Callout type="bug" title="Severity for accessibility bugs">
Rate <Term id="severity" /> by the impact on **affected users**, not on "most users". If a keyboard user can't complete checkout at all, that's a blocker for them, even if mouse users never notice. Many teams use WCAG level as a guide: level A failures are usually the most urgent.
</Callout>

What to listen for on every element: **name** ("Email"), **role** ("edit text", "button", "checkbox"), **state** ("checked", "expanded", "invalid data") and **value**. Custom components need <Term id="aria">ARIA</Term> to provide these, and ARIA used wrongly can make things worse than no ARIA at all.

### Automated checkers

Automated tools are fast and great at catching the obvious issues. Run one on every page you test:

- **<Term id="lighthouse" />** (built into Chrome <Term id="devtools" />, **Lighthouse** tab, check *Accessibility*): gives a score and a list of failed audits. Its accessibility checks use the axe engine.
- **axe DevTools** (free browser extension by Deque): adds an **axe DevTools** tab to DevTools with a detailed list of issues, the failing element and how to fix it.
- **WAVE** (free extension by WebAIM): draws icons directly on the page for missing alt text, contrast errors, empty buttons and the heading structure.
- **DevTools Elements › Accessibility pane**: shows the computed accessible name, role and ARIA attributes of any element you inspect.

<Callout type="warning" title="A 100 score doesn't mean accessible">
Automated tools can only detect **part** of WCAG failures: estimates usually range from roughly a third to about half. A tool can tell that an image *has* alt text, not whether the text makes sense. It can't tell whether focus order is logical, whether an error message is helpful, or whether a custom dropdown works with a keyboard. **Manual testing is always needed**, and a page can score 100 in Lighthouse and still be unusable with a keyboard.
</Callout>

### A quick manual routine (about 10 minutes per page)

<Steps>

1. **Run an automated scan** (Lighthouse or axe) and note real issues.
2. **Unplug the mouse.** Tab through the whole page and complete the main task.
3. **Zoom to 200% and 400%**, or set the window to 320 px wide.
4. **Check contrast** of anything faint, plus hover, focus and error states.
5. **Turn on a screen reader** and listen to the key flow: labels, buttons, errors.
6. **Turn on a greyscale filter** and check nothing depends on color alone.
7. **Check media and motion:** captions, pause buttons, Reduce motion.

</Steps>

<KeyTakeaways>

- Accessibility helps people with **permanent, temporary and situational** disabilities: about 1 in 6 people, and really everyone.
- **WCAG 2.2 level AA** is the usual target. Its four principles are **Perceivable, Operable, Understandable, Robust**.
- The most common failures: **missing labels, removed focus outlines, low contrast, color-only errors, missing alt text, unnamed icon buttons and custom controls that ignore the keyboard**.
- Test with your **keyboard**, **zoom**, a **contrast checker**, a **color filter** and a **screen reader**: VoiceOver, TalkBack or NVDA.
- Automated tools (Lighthouse, axe, WAVE) are a great first pass but catch **only part** of the problems.
- Report accessibility bugs with the **WCAG criterion**, the **assistive technology used**, and the impact on affected users.

</KeyTakeaways>
