It's Monday at a food delivery startup. Your lead drops a message in the team chat: *"Reorder ships next sprint. Can you put the testing together? Scenarios first, then whatever else you think we need."*

Scenarios? Test cases? Someone else on the team keeps talking about "the regression checklist". Are those three names for the same thing? (They're not.) This lesson sorts them out, so you know which one to write, how much detail to put in, and why.

## Three artifacts, three questions

Each artifact answers a different question:

| Artifact | Answers | Looks like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <Term id="test-scenario">Test scenario</Term> | **What** should we test? | One line: "Reorder when the restaurant is closed" |
| <Term id="test-case">Test case</Term> | **How** exactly, and what counts as a pass? | Preconditions, steps, data, expected result |
| <Term id="checklist">Checklist</Term> | What must I **remember** to check? | A list of short checks to tick, no steps |

<Callout type="example" title="Think of a trip">

A **scenario** is "visit Rome". A **test case** is the turn-by-turn route with the exact address and what the hotel looks like when you arrive. A **checklist** is the packing list you glance at before leaving: passport, charger, tickets. You need all three, just not for the same job.

</Callout>

## The essentials

### When to use which

- **Scenarios** to plan and agree on coverage with your team, fast. Always start here.
- **Test cases** where mistakes are costly or proof is needed: payments, banking, health, anything regulated. Also when **new people** will run the tests.
- **Checklists** for quick, repeated checks by people who know the app: a <Term id="smoke-testing">smoke</Term> round on each build, a quick <Term id="sanity-testing">sanity</Term> check after a small fix, a light regression pass, or reminders during <Term id="exploratory-testing" />.

### How they relate

**One scenario → several test cases.** "Reorder when some items are sold out" becomes: 1 of 3 items sold out, every item sold out, a sold-out item with extras. Checklists don't sit in that chain. They cut across it as quick reminders.

### From user story to tests in one line

Read the <Term id="user-story" /> and its <Term id="acceptance-criteria" /> ([how they work](/tutorials/user-stories-and-acceptance-criteria)) → list the scenarios (happy path, problems, limits) → turn the risky ones into test cases → collect quick checks into a checklist.

### How much detail?

Write enough that **someone who has never seen the app** gets the same Pass or Fail as you, and no more. For the full guide to writing a single case, see [How to Write Test Cases](/tutorials/writing-test-cases).

<Callout type="tip">

Most real teams **mix** the three. A typical pack: one page of scenarios, detailed cases for the risky 20%, and a checklist for everything you run on every build.

</Callout>

## Test scenarios in depth

A <Term id="test-scenario" /> is a **one-line description of a situation worth testing**. It names the user, the action and the twist, and stops there.

Good scenarios share three traits:

- **They describe a situation, not a click path.** "Reorder when the cart already has items from another restaurant", not "Tap Orders, tap Reorder".
- **They can be read by anyone.** Product owners, designers and developers can review a list of scenarios in five minutes and say "you forgot the closed-restaurant case".
- **They cover the unhappy paths too.** For every happy scenario, ask: what if something is missing, wrong, late, or at its limit?

<Compare>
<Do title="Clear scenarios">

- Reorder a delivered order with all items available
- Reorder when some items are sold out
- Reorder when prices have changed since the original order
- Reorder from a closed restaurant

</Do>
<Dont title="Too vague or too detailed">

- Test Reorder *(what about it?)*
- Reorder works *(that's a hope, not a scenario)*
- Tap Orders, tap Reorder on #1042, check 1 × Margherita *(that's already a test case)*

</Dont>
</Compare>

## Test cases in depth

A <Term id="test-case" /> turns one slice of a scenario into **exact instructions**: <Term id="precondition">preconditions</Term>, numbered steps, <Term id="test-data" /> and an <Term id="expected-result" /> that can only pass or fail. Everything about writing one well is in [How to Write Test Cases](/tutorials/writing-test-cases). Here we focus on one question that lesson leaves open: **how detailed should a case be?**

### How detailed should a test case be?

There's no single right answer, but there is a good rule: **detail should follow risk and the reader.**

| Situation | Detail level | Example step |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Regulated or money flow, new testers | High | "Tap **Reorder** on order #1042 (Luigi's, 3 May)" |
| Normal feature, team knows the app | Medium | "Reorder order #1042" |
| Low risk, experienced team | Low (maybe a checklist) | "Reorder works for a past order" |

Two tests for "enough detail":

1. **The new-tester test.** Could someone on their first day run it without asking you a question?
2. **The two-tester test.** Would two testers running it independently agree on Pass or Fail?

If either answer is no, add detail. If the case describes every pixel and breaks whenever a button moves, you've gone too far.

<Compare>
<Do title="Right amount of detail">

**Steps:** 1. Open the Orders tab. 2. Tap **Reorder** on order #1042.

**Expected:** Cart shows 1 × Margherita (no olives) at today's price, with the message "2 items are no longer available".

</Do>
<Dont title="Over-specified">

**Steps:** 1. Tap the third icon from the left in the bottom bar (grey clock). 2. Scroll down 240 px. 3. Tap the green button in the top-right of the second card.

**Expected:** Total is exactly 11.90 €.

</Dont>
</Compare>

The bad example breaks the moment the designer changes a color or the restaurant changes a price, even though nothing is wrong with Reorder.

## Checklists in depth

A <Term id="checklist" /> is a list of short checks **without steps or detailed expected results**. It trusts the tester to know how to get there and what "right" looks like. That's what makes it fast to write, fast to run and cheap to update.

Checklists come in two main flavors:

- **Smoke and regression checklists.** The 10 to 30 things you check on every build or before every release (see [Regression and Smoke Suites](/tutorials/regression-and-smoke-suites)), such as "Reorder button shows on delivered orders" or "Cart badge updates".
- **Heuristics checklists.** Reminders of *kinds* of things that often break, used while [exploring](/tutorials/exploratory-testing): rotate the phone, interrupt with a call, go offline, switch to <Term id="dark-mode" />, turn on large text, try a <Term id="screen-reader" />.

<Compare>
<Do title="Checkable items">

- Sold-out items are skipped, not added at 0 €
- Extras such as "no onions" are kept
- Reorder button is at least 44 × 44 pt

</Do>
<Dont title="Not checkable">

- Reorder is good
- Check everything about the cart
- Make sure there are no bugs

</Dont>
</Compare>

<Callout type="warning" title="The checklist trap">

A checklist only works if the person running it knows the app. "Extras are kept" means nothing to someone who has never added an extra. When you hand a checklist to someone new, pair them up for the first run or link each item to a test case.

</Callout>

## How they fit together

<StoryTree />

- A **user story** gives you a handful of **scenarios**.
- Each scenario produces **two or more test cases**: the happy one, the negative ones, the limits.
- **Checklists** run alongside: a smoke checklist for every build, a heuristics checklist for exploratory sessions.

Linking each test case back to its scenario and story is what makes a <Term id="traceability-matrix" /> possible: you can show which requirement is covered by which tests.

## When to use which

| Context | Lean towards | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Banking, health, regulated | Detailed test cases | Auditors want evidence; mistakes are costly |
| Fast startup, frequent releases | Scenarios + checklists | Cheap to write and update as the app changes |
| New testers on the team | Detailed test cases | They teach the product and keep results consistent |
| Experienced testers | Checklists | They know the "how"; save time for exploring |
| Regression pack run often | Cases for risky flows, checklist for the rest | Balance repeatability and upkeep |
| Exploratory session | A short goal + heuristics checklist | Freedom to follow your curiosity |

<Callout type="note" title="Honest note">

Teams use these words loosely. Some call a list of scenarios a "checklist"; some call any spreadsheet row a "test case". Don't argue about the label. Ask: *does this have steps and an expected result?* That tells you what you're really looking at.

</Callout>

## Worked example: from user story to scenarios to cases

Here's the full journey for FoodDash's **Reorder** feature.

<Callout type="info" title="User story: FoodDash Reorder">

*As a returning customer, I want to reorder a previous order from my order history so I can get my usual meal in two taps.*

**Acceptance criteria**

1. Delivered orders from the **last 90 days** show a **Reorder** button.
2. Tapping Reorder adds all available items, **with the same extras**, to the cart and opens the cart.
3. Items that are no longer available are skipped, and the app shows "N items are no longer available".
4. **Today's prices** are used. If the total differs from the original order, a note says so.
5. If the restaurant is closed, Reorder is disabled and shows "Closed · opens at 11:00".
6. If the cart already has items from another restaurant, the app asks "Start a new cart?" before replacing it.

</Callout>

<Steps>

1. **Read the story and mark every rule.** Six criteria, three numbers or limits (90 days, N items, opening time) and three messages. Write down questions: does "last 90 days" include day 90? What happens if *every* item is sold out?
2. **List the scenarios.** One per rule at least, plus the unhappy and edge situations you can think of:
   - SC-RO-01 Reorder a delivered order with all items available
   - SC-RO-02 Reorder when some items are sold out
   - SC-RO-03 Reorder when prices changed since the original order
   - SC-RO-04 Reorder from a closed restaurant
   - SC-RO-05 Reorder when the cart has items from another restaurant
   - SC-RO-06 Reorder button visibility (age of order, canceled orders)
3. **Review the scenarios with the team.** Share the list in refinement. The product owner answers: day 90 is included; canceled orders never show Reorder; if every item is sold out, the cart doesn't open and the message reads "None of these items are available right now".
4. **Turn the risky scenarios into test cases.** Money, data and confusing states get full cases. Below are four of them.
5. **Collect the quick checks into a checklist.** UI and platform details that an experienced tester can check in seconds.

</Steps>

### The Reorder checklist

- Reorder button visible on delivered orders only
- Button readable in light and dark mode
- Button is at least 44 × 44 pt (iOS) / 48 × 48 dp (Android)
- Screen reader announces "Reorder, order from Luigi's, 3 May"
- Cart badge shows the new item count
- Price-change note appears when the total differs
- Double-tapping Reorder doesn't add the items twice
- Works after rotating and after returning from the background
- Offline: a clear "No connection" message, nothing half-added

<Callout type="tip" title="Notice the split">

The four cases cover money, data and confusing states, where a wrong guess costs real customers. The checklist covers things an experienced tester can see at a glance. Same feature, two artifacts, each doing what it's best at.

</Callout>

## Keeping them maintainable

Tests that nobody updates turn into noise: they fail for the wrong reasons, so people stop trusting them. A few habits keep your tests useful:

- **Name things by label, not by position or color.** "Tap **Reorder**", not "tap the green button on the right".
- **Don't hard-code values that change.** Prefer "today's price for Margherita" over "11.90 €", unless the exact number *is* what you're testing.
- **Keep data in preconditions or test data, not buried in steps.** It's easier to update one line than to hunt through ten steps.
- **Link every case to its scenario and story.** When the story changes, you instantly know which cases to review.
- **Prune.** When a feature is removed or redesigned, archive the old cases. A pack of 300 cases where 80 are outdated is worse than a pack of 220 that are all correct.
- **Promote and demote.** A checklist item that keeps finding bugs deserves a full case. A case that has passed unchanged for a year in a low-risk area can shrink to a checklist line.

## Common mistakes

<Callout type="bug" title="Watch out for these">

- **Jumping straight to test cases.** Without a scenario list you can't see gaps, and nobody can review your coverage quickly.
- **Writing scenarios that are really test cases.** If a scenario has steps or exact data, it's a case in disguise.
- **Checklists with uncheckable items.** "Reorder is good" can't be ticked. Every item should have an obvious yes or no.
- **Same detail everywhere.** Full cases for a tooltip text, a vague checklist line for the payment total. Match detail to risk.
- **Handing a checklist to a newcomer** without explaining the app first.
- **Never updating.** Outdated tests cause false failures and slowly destroy trust in the whole pack.

</Callout>

<KeyTakeaways>

- A **scenario** says what to test in one line, a **test case** says exactly how and what counts as a pass, and a **checklist** is a list of quick checks without steps.
- Start with scenarios: they're fast to write and easy for the whole team to review.
- One scenario becomes several test cases. Checklists run alongside, for smoke, regression and exploratory reminders.
- Match detail to risk and reader: detailed cases for money, regulated flows and newcomers; checklists for experienced testers and frequent checks.
- Keep tests maintainable: use labels not positions, avoid hard-coded values, link to stories, and prune old ones.

</KeyTakeaways>
