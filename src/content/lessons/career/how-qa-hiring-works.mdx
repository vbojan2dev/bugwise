A friend forwards you a job ad: *"Junior QA Tester, mobile and web. Hybrid. Apply by Friday."* You click Apply, upload your CV, and then... what happens? Who reads it? How many interviews are there? Will they make you test something live?

Hiring can feel like a black box, especially when you are changing careers. It isn't. Most companies follow a similar path, and every stage checks something specific. Once you know what each step is looking for, you can prepare for it on purpose instead of hoping for the best.

<Callout type="note" title="Every company is different">

What follows is a **typical** process for junior manual QA roles. A five-person startup might hire you after one chat with the founder; a bank or a big outsourcing company might have six rounds and a written test. The order, the number of rounds and even the names change by company and by country. Use this as a map, not a promise.

</Callout>

## The six stages at a glance

| # | Stage | What it mainly checks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Application and ATS | Does your CV match the job? |
| 2 | Recruiter or HR screen | Motivation, communication, logistics |
| 3 | Technical interview | Testing knowledge and thinking |
| 4 | Practical task | How you actually test |
| 5 | Team or culture fit | Working with others |
| 6 | Offer | The deal itself |

Stages 3 and 4 are often combined ("we'll ask a few questions, then you'll test something"), and stage 5 can be a separate round or part of another one. From application to offer, the whole thing often takes a few weeks, but it can be faster or much slower.

<Short>

## The essentials

### 1. Application and ATS

Many companies use an **ATS** (applicant tracking system): software that collects applications and lets recruiters search and filter them. Your CV may be found (or not) by keywords from the job ad, so use the ad's words where they are honestly true for you: *manual testing, test cases, bug reports, Jira, mobile, API*. Keep the layout simple so the software can read it. See [A QA CV and Portfolio Without Experience](/career/cv-and-portfolio-without-experience).

### 2. Recruiter or HR screen (15 to 30 minutes)

A friendly call about why you applied, your background, when you could start, where you can work and your salary expectations. Prepare a [60 to 90 second introduction](/career/tell-me-about-yourself-and-why-qa), one honest sentence about why QA, and something specific about the company.

### 3. Technical interview (45 to 60 minutes)

A tester or QA lead asks about basics (<Term id="severity" /> vs <Term id="priority" />, <Term id="regression-testing" />, how to write a <Term id="bug-report" />) and gives you small "how would you test..." problems. Practice on the [Interview Prep](/interview) page out loud, with examples.

### 4. Practical task

Either **live** (share your screen and test a login page, a search box or "this pen") or a **take-home** ("test this website and send bug reports in 48 hours"). They want to see your thinking: where you start, which cases you pick, how clearly you write. See [Take-Home Assignments](/career/take-home-assignments).

### 5. Team or culture fit

You meet people you would work with. Expect behavioral questions such as *"Tell me about a time you disagreed with someone"*. Answer with a short, real story.

### 6. Offer

You get salary, start date, probation and benefits, ideally in writing. Read it carefully, ask questions, and negotiate politely if it makes sense.

### Always ask questions back

At the end, you'll hear *"Do you have any questions for us?"* Have two or three ready, for example: *"How do testers and developers work together here?"* or *"What would a good first three months look like?"* See [the model answer](/interview/questions-for-the-interviewer).

## Stage 1: the application and the ATS

**What happens.** You apply through a company's career page, a job board or LinkedIn. Most mid-size and large companies use an **applicant tracking system (ATS)**: software that stores every application, lets recruiters search by keywords and filter by answers like location or work permit, and tracks where each candidate is in the process.

**What it checks.** Whether you match the basics of the role closely enough for a person to take a look. It is less of a robot rejecting you than a search engine: if a recruiter searches for "Jira" and "mobile testing" and your CV says neither, you simply don't show up.

**How to prepare.**

- **Mirror the ad's words where they are true.** If the ad says "test cases" and "bug reports", say exactly that, not only "quality checks".
- **Use a simple layout.** One column, standard headings (Experience, Projects, Skills, Education), a normal font, and a PDF or Word file as the ad asks. Text inside images, tables and fancy columns can be read badly by some systems.
- **Answer the knockout questions honestly.** "Do you have the right to work in this country?" and "Can you work from our office two days a week?" are filters. A false answer only wastes everyone's time later.
- **Tailor, but don't rewrite everything.** Adjust your summary and the order of your skills for each ad. Ten good applications usually beat a hundred identical ones.

<Callout type="tip" title="Referrals help a lot">

An application recommended by someone inside the company usually gets read by a person. Testing meetups, online QA communities and former colleagues who now work in tech are worth more than one more click on "Easy Apply".

</Callout>

## Stage 2: the recruiter or HR screen

**What happens.** A 15 to 30 minute call, often by video, with a recruiter. They may be an in-house recruiter or work for an agency. They are usually not testers.

**What it checks.**

- **Motivation:** do you actually want this job, and do you know what a tester does?
- **Communication:** can you explain things clearly and briefly? For a tester this matters a lot, because you will explain bugs all day.
- **Logistics:** start date or notice period, location and remote work, work permit, language, salary expectations.

**How to prepare.**

<Steps>

1. **Re-read the job ad and the company's website.** Know what product they make and who uses it. Install their app if they have one and use it for 15 minutes.
2. **Prepare your 60 to 90 second introduction** (present, past, future). There's a builder in [Tell Me About Yourself and Why QA](/career/tell-me-about-yourself-and-why-qa).
3. **Prepare one honest answer to "Why QA?"** with a small story, not "I like computers".
4. **Research a salary range** for junior testers in your city or country before the call, and decide how you'll answer. See [What are your salary expectations?](/interview/salary-expectations).
5. **Have questions ready** about the next steps: "What does the rest of the process look like?"

</Steps>

## Stage 3: the technical interview

**What happens.** A 45 to 60 minute conversation with a tester, QA lead or engineering manager. Expect a mix of:

- **Concept questions:** <Term id="severity" /> vs <Term id="priority" />, <Term id="retesting" /> vs <Term id="regression-testing" />, <Term id="smoke-testing" />, the <Term id="bug-life-cycle" />, <Term id="test-case" /> vs <Term id="test-scenario" />, <Term id="equivalence-partitioning" /> and <Term id="boundary-value-analysis" />.
- **Scenario questions:** *"What do you do when a developer says it's not a bug?"*, *"There isn't time to test everything. What now?"*
- **Mobile and web basics** if the role mentions them: <Term id="interruption">interruptions</Term>, <Term id="orientation" />, permissions, <Term id="devtools" />, <Term id="cookie">cookies</Term>, <Term id="http-status-code">status codes</Term>.
- **Small design tasks:** *"How would you test a search box?"* (sometimes this is the practical task).

**What it checks.** Not whether you can recite definitions, but whether you **understand** them well enough to give an example, and whether you think like a tester: curious, structured, focused on risk.

**How to prepare.**

- Go through the junior questions on the [Interview Prep](/interview) page **out loud**. Explaining something to an empty room is harder than reading it, and that's the point.
- For each concept, prepare **one example** from a real app: a banking app, a food delivery app, the Capstone.
- Revisit the fundamentals you're least sure of. [Severity vs Priority](/fundamentals/severity-vs-priority), [Types of Testing](/fundamentals/types-of-testing) and [Bug Life Cycle](/fundamentals/bug-life-cycle) come up constantly.
- It's fine to say *"I haven't used that yet, but here's how I would approach it."* It's not fine to bluff.

## Stage 4: the practical task

Many QA processes include a hands-on task, because watching someone test tells you more than any question. There are two common forms.

### Live tasks

You share your screen or stand at a whiteboard and work through a problem while thinking aloud. The most common ones:

| Task | What a strong answer covers |
| --- | --- |
| "Test this pen" (or a lift, a chair, a coffee machine) | Ask questions first, then functional, negative, usability, durability, safety cases |
| "Test this login page" | Valid login, wrong password, empty fields, lockout, password reset, security and accessibility |
| "How would you test a search box?" | Exact, partial, no results, special characters, long input, speed, filters |
| "Write test cases for X" | A few clear, prioritized cases with steps and expected results |
| "Find bugs on this page" | Calm exploration, then clear reports of the worst issues first |

Each has a model answer on the Interview Prep page: [a pen or a lift](/interview/how-to-test-a-pen-or-a-lift), [a login screen](/interview/how-to-test-a-login-screen), [a search box](/interview/how-to-test-a-search-box), [a shopping cart](/interview/how-to-test-a-shopping-cart) and [a date picker](/interview/how-to-test-a-date-picker).

**A plan that works for almost any live task:**

<Steps>

1. **Ask clarifying questions.** *"Who uses it? On which devices? Are there requirements? How much time do I have?"* Interviewers often hide the most important information until you ask.
2. **Say your plan out loud.** *"I'll start with the main happy path, then negative cases, then edge cases, then non-functional things like accessibility and performance."*
3. **Start with what matters most.** The core flow and the risky parts (money, data, login) before colors and typos.
4. **Think aloud.** Silence makes interviewers guess. *"I'm trying a password with a space at the end, because phones sometimes add one."*
5. **Write things down clearly.** If you find a bug, describe it like a short [bug report](/tutorials/writing-bug-reports): steps, expected, actual.
6. **Wrap up.** *"In 20 minutes I covered A and B. With more time I'd look at C and D."*

</Steps>

### Take-home tasks

*"Here's a link to our staging site. Please test the checkout and send us bug reports and a short summary within 48 hours."* This is common enough to have its own lesson: [Take-Home Assignments](/career/take-home-assignments).

**What practical tasks check.** How you prioritize, how you think about risk, whether you cover negative and edge cases, and how clearly you communicate. A candidate who finds five important bugs and explains them well usually beats one who lists fifty typos.

## Stage 5: team or culture fit

**What happens.** You meet future teammates, often developers and a product owner, sometimes a manager. It may be a separate round or the last part of another one.

**What it checks.** Collaboration, how you handle conflict and feedback, how you learn, and whether you would add something to the team. "Culture fit" should mean shared ways of working, not "people just like us".

**How to prepare.** Have three or four short, true stories ready using **STAR** (Situation, Task, Action, Result), for example a time you:

- found a problem others missed (it doesn't have to be in software),
- disagreed with someone and handled it well,
- learned something new quickly,
- made a mistake and fixed it.

There are model answers for [disagreeing with a teammate](/interview/disagreed-with-a-teammate), [learning from a mistake](/interview/learning-from-a-mistake), [learning a new product quickly](/interview/learning-a-new-product-quickly) and [a developer saying "it's not a bug"](/interview/developer-says-not-a-bug).

## Stage 6: the offer

**What happens.** Someone, usually the recruiter, calls with an offer, then sends it in writing.

**What to check.** Salary (gross or net, monthly or yearly), start date, contract type and length, probation period, working hours, remote or office days, holidays, benefits, notice period, and anything about training or certification budgets.

**How to handle it.**

- Say thank you and ask for it **in writing** before you accept.
- It is normal to ask for a day or two to decide.
- If the salary is below what your research suggests, you can ask politely once, with reasons. Many junior offers have some room; some don't. Both are normal.
- If you get a "no" at any stage, ask (politely) for feedback. Not every company gives it, but when they do, it is gold.

## Questions to ask the interviewer

*"Do you have any questions for us?"* is not a formality. Good questions show you're serious about testing, and the answers tell you whether you want the job. Pick two or three per interview, and don't ask what the website already says.

**About the work**

- *"What does a typical week look like for a tester here?"*
- *"How do testers and developers work together? When does QA see a feature for the first time?"*
- *"Is testing mostly manual, automated or both? Is there a path toward automation for someone who's interested?"*

**About the team and growth**

- *"How many testers are on the team, and who would I learn from?"*
- *"What would a great first three months look like for this role?"*
- *"How do you handle bugs that are found just before a release?"*

**About the process**

- *"What are the next steps, and when can I expect to hear back?"*

The model answer to [Do you have questions for us?](/interview/questions-for-the-interviewer) has more, plus the questions to avoid early on.

<KeyTakeaways>

- A typical QA hiring process has six stages: application and ATS, recruiter screen, technical interview, practical task, team fit and offer. Companies and countries vary a lot.
- Each stage checks something different, so prepare for each on purpose: keywords, pitch, concepts with examples, a live-task plan, STAR stories, an offer checklist.
- In live tasks, ask questions first, say your plan, start with the riskiest areas, think aloud and wrap up with what you did and didn't cover.
- Practical tasks reward prioritization and clear communication, not the number of bugs.
- Always have two or three questions ready for the interviewer. Their answers help you decide too.

</KeyTakeaways>
