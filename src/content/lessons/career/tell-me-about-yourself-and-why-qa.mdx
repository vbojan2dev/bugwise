The video call connects. The interviewer smiles, introduces herself, and says: *"So, tell me a little about yourself."*

It's the most predictable question in any interview, and still the one people ramble through. In the first two minutes you set the tone for everything after. A clear, confident answer makes the interviewer curious; a nervous life story makes them check the time.

The good news: because these opening questions are so predictable, they're the easiest to prepare. This lesson gives you a structure and templates for the five you'll almost certainly hear:

1. *Tell me about yourself.*
2. *Why do you want to work in QA?*
3. *Why our company?*
4. *What are your salary expectations?*
5. *You don't have any experience in testing. Why should we hire you?*

<Short>

## The essentials

### "Tell me about yourself": present, past, future

Aim for **60 to 90 seconds**, about 140 to 210 words spoken calmly.

| Part | Say | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Present | Who you are now and what you're doing toward QA | "I'm a support specialist training to become a manual tester..." |
| Past | One relevant strength with a concrete example | "In support I reproduce app problems and write tickets for developers..." |
| Future | What you want and why this role | "Now I'm looking for a junior QA role in a product team like yours..." |

Not your full CV, not your childhood, not your hobbies (unless they're relevant).

### "Why QA?"

A short, true story beats a general statement. What moment made you realize you like finding problems, understanding why things break or making things work for users?

### "Why our company?"

Something **specific** you found: their product, their users, how their team works. Use the app before the interview.

### Salary expectations

Research a realistic range for junior testers in your city or country before any call. Early in the process, you can politely ask for their range first. If they insist, give your researched range. Be honest, never invent a current salary.

### "You have no experience"

Agree calmly, then show evidence: your practice project, transferable skills and how fast you learn. No apologies.

## Why the opening questions matter

The interviewer isn't really asking for your biography. They're checking:

- **Can you communicate clearly and briefly?** Testers explain problems all day, to developers, product owners and sometimes customers.
- **Do you know what the job is?** "I like computers" suggests you don't.
- **Is your move into QA a real choice?** Especially for career changers.
- **What should I ask next?** Your pitch gives them hooks. A good one leads to questions you're ready for.

## "Tell me about yourself"

### The structure: present, past, future

<Steps>

1. **Present (about 20 seconds).** Who you are right now and what you're doing toward testing. *"I'm a customer support specialist at a travel booking company, and for the last six months I've been training to become a manual tester. I've finished a testing course and a practice project where I tested a food delivery app end to end."*
2. **Past (about 30 seconds).** The one or two things from your background that make you a good tester, with a concrete example. *"In support, a big part of my day is reproducing problems customers report and writing clear tickets for developers. Last year I noticed that several failed-booking complaints came from older Android phones. I collected the details, and the team found a bug in the payment screen."*
3. **Future (about 20 seconds).** What you want and why this role. *"Now I'm looking for a junior QA role in a product team. Your app is used by people on the go, so mobile quality matters a lot, and I like that your testers work inside the development teams."*

</Steps>

Then stop. Silence after a good answer is fine. The interviewer will pick a thread.

<Compare>
<Do title="Focused">

Present, then one past example that shows tester skills, then why this job. About 70 seconds. Ends with the role.

</Do>
<Dont title="Unfocused">

"Well, I was born in Porto, I studied biology, then I moved to Berlin, I worked in a café for a while, then in support, and I don't really have experience, but I like technology and I think QA is a good way into IT..."

</Dont>
</Compare>

<Callout type="tip" title="Say it out loud, don't memorize it word for word">

Write it, then practice speaking it from three bullet points: present, past example, future. A memorized script sounds recited; bullet points keep it natural. Record yourself once on your phone and listen back. It's uncomfortable and very effective.

</Callout>

## "Why do you want to work in QA?"

What they're checking: that you understand what testers do and chose it on purpose, not as "an easy way into IT".

**A strong answer has three parts:**

1. **A true moment or pattern** that drew you to testing: a bug you found, a process you fixed, a product that frustrated you.
2. **What you enjoy about the work itself:** investigating, thinking like a user, finding the cause, making things reliable.
3. **What you've done about it:** the course, the practice project, the open-source issue you filed.

<Compare>
<Do title="Real and specific">

"In retail I was the one who noticed when the till prices didn't match the shelf labels, and I'd track down why. When I learned that testing is basically that, for software, I took a course and tested a practice app end to end. The part I enjoy most is narrowing a vague problem down to exact steps someone can fix."

</Do>
<Dont title="Weak or risky">

- "QA is easier than programming, so it's a good way into IT."
- "I like computers and technology."
- "I want to do QA for a year and then move to development." (Fine to grow, but leading with leaving is a red flag.)

</Dont>
</Compare>

## "Why our company?"

What they're checking: that you applied to **them**, not to every ad on the board.

**Prepare in 30 minutes:**

- **Use their product.** Install the app or use the website for 15 minutes like a customer. Note one or two things you liked and, carefully, one question.
- **Read the job ad again** for clues about how they work: "testers embedded in squads", "release every week", "mobile-first".
- **Look at their careers page, engineering blog or LinkedIn posts** for values, team size and tech.
- **Check app store reviews.** They show what real users love and complain about. Mention them tactfully, if at all.

**Template:**

> *"I tried your [app/site] before this call. I liked [specific thing], and as a tester I noticed [respectful observation or question]. I'm also interested in [something about how they work, from the ad or blog], because [why it fits you]."*

<Callout type="warning" title="Careful with pointing out bugs">

Finding a bug in their app can be a nice talking point, but frame it as curiosity, not criticism: *"I noticed the search didn't find 'cafe' without the accent. Is that something the team knows about?"* Never test their production systems aggressively to find something to say. See the ethics section in [Take-Home Assignments](/career/take-home-assignments).

</Callout>

## "What are your salary expectations?"

This question makes most people nervous, mostly because they haven't prepared for it. Prepare, and it becomes a normal part of the conversation.

### Step 1: research a range before any call

Salaries for junior testers vary hugely by country, city, company size, industry and remote policy, so there is no single right number, and we won't give one here. Instead, build your own range from several sources:

- **Job ads with published salary ranges.** Some countries and US states require employers to share a pay range in the job ad or before the interview; where that applies, it's your best source.
- **Salary surveys and comparison sites** for your country, and official statistics where they exist.
- **People:** testers in local meetups or online communities, friends in IT, and recruiters, who usually know the market well.

Write down a realistic range for a **junior** role, decide the lowest number you'd accept, and know whether you're talking gross or net, monthly or yearly, as is usual where you live.

### Step 2: early in the process, ask for their range first

In a first recruiter call, it's polite and common to ask for their range before you give a number:

> *"I'd like to understand the role a bit better first. Could you share the range you've budgeted for this position?"*

Many recruiters will tell you. If the range fits, say so: *"That range works for me, depending on the full package."*

### Step 3: if they insist, give your researched range

> *"Based on what I've seen for junior QA roles in [city/country], I'm looking at something between [X] and [Y], depending on the full package and the growth opportunities."*

A range with your realistic target near its lower end is common practice. Don't name a number far above the market for juniors, and don't undersell yourself out of nervousness either.

### Step 4: be honest

- **Never invent a current salary or a competing offer.** It can be checked, and it destroys trust.
- In some places, employers aren't allowed to ask about your salary history. You can still choose to share it or politely decline: *"I'd rather focus on what's fair for this role."*
- If an offer comes in lower than your research suggests, you can ask once, politely, with reasons. See the offer stage in [How QA Hiring Works](/career/how-qa-hiring-works).

## "You don't have any experience"

Sometimes it's a question, sometimes a statement, sometimes a test of how you react. Either way: **don't apologize, don't argue, show evidence.**

**A four-part answer:**

1. **Agree honestly.** *"That's right, I haven't worked as a tester yet."*
2. **Reframe with real work.** *"But I've done the work of testing on a practice project: I planned it, wrote test cases, filed bug reports and wrote a release recommendation. I can show you."*
3. **Bring transferable experience.** *"And in five years of support I reproduced customer problems and wrote tickets developers could act on every day."*
4. **Show you learn fast.** *"I went from zero to that project in six months while working full-time, and I'd bring the same energy to learning your product."*

<Compare>
<Do title="Confident and factual">

"That's true, I haven't had a QA job yet. What I do have is a complete practice project, a portfolio with my test plan and bug reports, and years of reproducing customer problems in support. I'd love to walk you through one of my bug reports."

</Do>
<Dont title="Apologetic or defensive">

"Yes, sorry, I know I only did a course and I don't really have experience, but I'm a fast learner and I really want this."

</Dont>
</Compare>

## Fill-in templates

**Tell me about yourself**

```text
PRESENT: I'm a <current role or situation>, and for the last <time> I've been
<what you've done toward QA: course, practice project, open-source bugs>.

PAST: Before that / in my current job, I <tester-like task you did often>.
For example, <one short concrete story with a result>.

FUTURE: Now I'm looking for <type of QA role>. <Company> appeals to me because
<specific reason>, and I'd bring <your strength>.
```

**Why QA?**

```text
I realized I enjoy <investigating / getting details right / thinking like the user>
when <true moment>. Testing is exactly that, for software. So I <what you did about it>,
and the part I enjoy most is <specific activity>.
```

**Why our company?**

```text
I used <product> before this call and liked <specific thing>. I also noticed
<question or observation, tactfully>. And I like that your team <how they work>,
because <why it fits you>.
```

**Salary expectations**

```text
Early: "Could you share the range budgeted for this role?"
If pressed: "For junior QA roles in <place>, I'm looking at <X> to <Y>,
depending on the full package and growth opportunities."
```

**No experience**

```text
That's right, I haven't worked as a tester yet. But I <practice project + deliverables>,
and in <past job> I <transferable skill with example>. I learned all this in <time>
while <circumstance>, and I'd love to show you <portfolio piece>.
```

</Long>

<KeyTakeaways>

- The opening questions are predictable, so prepare them on purpose.
- "Tell me about yourself": 60 to 90 seconds of present, past (one concrete example) and future. Practice from bullet points.
- "Why QA?": a true moment, what you enjoy about the work, and what you did about it.
- "Why us?": something specific from using their product and reading how they work.
- Salary: research a realistic range first, ask for theirs early, give yours if pressed, and never invent numbers.
- "No experience": agree, show evidence, connect transferable skills, and don't apologize.

</KeyTakeaways>
