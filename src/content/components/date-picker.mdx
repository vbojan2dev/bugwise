Booking a hotel, scheduling a food delivery, setting an alarm, filtering bank transactions by month: they all need a date or a time. The control you use to choose it is a <Term id="date-picker">date picker</Term> (or time picker), and it is one of the **most bug-prone components** in any app.

Why? Because dates look simple but aren't. Months have 28 to 31 days. Some years have a February 29th. Half the world writes 03/04 for the 3rd of April, the other half for March 4th. Clocks jump forward and back once a year. And a server in one <Term id="time-zone">time zone</Term> talks to phones in all the others. Each of those facts has caused real bugs in real products. Let's learn how to find them.

## What is a date picker?

A date picker is any control that lets the user **choose a calendar date**, and a time picker lets them **choose a time of day**. Many apps combine the two. They come in a few shapes:

- **Calendar grid**: a month view where you tap a day. The most common shape on every platform.
- **Wheels**: spinning columns for month, day and year (or hour and minute). The classic iOS look.
- **Clock dial**: tap the hour on a clock face, then the minutes (Android).
- **Typed input**: a <Term id="text-field">text field</Term> with a format such as `DD/MM/YYYY`, often with a calendar button next to it.
- **Range picker**: one calendar for a *start* and *end* date, such as check-in and check-out on a hotel site.

### How it differs from similar input methods

There are many ways to ask for a date, and choosing the **wrong one** is a real usability finding.

| Input method | Best for | Weak at | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Calendar grid** | Dates close to today, where the weekday matters | Dates far away (birth dates) | Delivery date, appointment |
| **Range picker** | A start and an end that belong together | Single dates | Hotel stay, car rental, report period |
| **Wheel picker** | Times; dates when the exact weekday doesn't matter | Seeing the month as a whole; speed | Alarm time, reminder |
| **Typed field** | Dates users know by heart; power users | Ambiguous formats, typos | Card expiry, date of birth |
| **Three separate fields** | Memorable dates (birth, passport issue) | Picking relative to today | Date of birth in a government form |
| **Dropdowns** | A short list of fixed options | Anything with 31 options per field | "Next 7 days" delivery slots |

<Callout type="dev" title="Platform names">
Apple calls it a **date picker** (`UIDatePicker` / SwiftUI `DatePicker`) with styles **compact**, **inline** (graphical) and **wheels**. Google's Material 3 has **date pickers** (docked, modal, modal input) and **time pickers** (dial and input). On the web there is the native `<input type="date">` and countless custom calendar libraries.
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Typing vs picking">
Picking is safer (you can't pick February 30th) but slow for dates far away. Typing is fast but invites format mistakes. The best pickers offer **both**: Material's pencil icon, a text field next to a calendar, or the native web input, which lets you type each part. When both exist, **test both paths**. They often run different validation code.
</Callout>

## When to use it

A calendar or wheel picker is a good choice when:

- The date is **close to today** (next week's delivery, a table reservation this weekend).
- The **weekday matters**: users want to see that the 23rd is a Friday.
- Some dates are **unavailable** and should be visibly blocked (sold-out nights, past dates, closed days).
- You need a **range** where the user wants to see both ends together.
- For times: the choice is **one of a limited set** (every 15 minutes) or a familiar clock face helps.

## Common bugs

Date and time bugs are so common that many teams keep a dedicated checklist for them. These are the classics:

| # | Bug | What you see | Typical severity |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Impossible range** | Check-out before check-in, or an end date before the start date, is accepted | High |
| 2 | **Off-by-one count** | "2 nights" for a one-night stay; a 7-day report includes 8 days | High (wrong price) |
| 3 | **Impossible dates accepted** | Typed 31/04 or 29/02/2027 is accepted, or silently becomes 1 May / 1 March | Medium–High |
| 4 | **Day shifts by one** | The saved date is one day earlier or later than chosen, often only for users east or west of UTC, or around midnight | High |
| 5 | **Wrong format for the locale** | A UK user sees 03/04/2026 meaning March 4th; dates are typed one way and parsed another | High |
| 6 | **Wrong first day of week** | The week starts on Sunday for European users, or weekday labels don't line up with the dates | Medium |
| 7 | **Month navigation skips** | From January 31st, "next" jumps to March because "February 31st" doesn't exist | Medium |
| 8 | **Past or blocked dates selectable** | Sold-out or past days look disabled but can still be tapped or typed | High |
| 9 | **DST problems** | A 02:30 alarm on the day clocks spring forward never rings, or a 01:30 reminder fires twice in autumn | Medium–High |
| 10 | **12h / 24h mix-ups** | "12:30 AM" stored as midday, or the device's 24-hour setting is ignored | Medium |
| 11 | **Leap year bugs** | February 29th missing in 2028, or yearly renewals of Feb 29 crash in 2029 | Medium |
| 12 | **Cancel doesn't cancel** | In a modal picker, the date changes even after tapping Cancel | Medium |

<Callout type="bug" title="Why #4 happens: dates are not instants">
A *calendar date* ("October 20") is not a moment in time. Many apps turn it into "midnight on October 20 **in the phone's time zone**", save that as a UTC timestamp, and later turn it back into a date. Midnight in Belgrade is still October 19 in UTC and in New York. If any step uses a different zone, the date moves by a day. To catch it: pick a date, change the device <Term id="time-zone" />, and look again. Also try times near midnight.
</Callout>

## Stress-test it: locales and time zones

Most date bugs live in three places: **formats**, **time zones** and **boundary dates**. A little theory first, then the playground.

### The same date, written four ways

The **4th of March 2026** in the short date format of four <Term id="locale">locales</Term> (exact punctuation can vary slightly between browsers):

| Locale | Short date | Long date | 9:30 in the evening |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **en-US** (United States) | 3/4/26 | Wednesday, March 4, 2026 | 9:30 PM |
| **en-GB** (United Kingdom) | 04/03/2026 | Wednesday, 4 March 2026 | 21:30 |
| **de-DE** (Germany) | 04.03.26 | Mittwoch, 4. März 2026 | 21:30 |
| **sr-Latn** (Serbia) | 4. 3. 2026. | sreda, 4. mart 2026. | 21:30 |

Notice that **en-US and en-GB use the same digits in a different order**. "03/04" is March 4th in one and April 3rd in the other. A date that is typed in one format and parsed in another is a classic, high-severity <Term id="localization" /> bug, because it doesn't crash: it books the wrong day.

### First day of the week

The US calendar starts on **Sunday**. Most of Europe, including the UK, Germany and Serbia, starts on **Monday** (the ISO 8601 standard). Some countries in the Middle East start on **Saturday**. A calendar that ignores this isn't broken, but it feels wrong to users and causes mis-taps. A calendar where the **labels and the dates don't line up** is broken.

### 12-hour vs 24-hour clock

The locale picks a default, but users can override it (iOS: *Settings › General › Date & Time › 24-Hour Time*, Android: *Use 24-hour format*). Test both. Watch out for **12:00 AM** (midnight) and **12:00 PM** (noon): they are easy to get backwards.

### Time zones and daylight saving time

A time is only meaningful together with a time zone. "23:30 in Belgrade" is already the next day in Tokyo and still the same afternoon in New York. On top of that, many zones use **daylight saving time (DST)**:

- In **spring** the clocks jump forward (in the EU on the last Sunday of March, in the US on the second Sunday of March). One hour, such as 02:00–02:59, **doesn't exist** that night.
- In **autumn** they go back (EU: last Sunday of October, US: first Sunday of November). One hour **happens twice**.
- Some zones have **no DST** (Tokyo, Kolkata), some are offset by **half an hour** (Kolkata is UTC+5:30), and in the southern hemisphere (Auckland) the seasons, and so the changes, are reversed.

<Callout type="example" title="Boundary dates every tester should know">
These are the <Term id="boundary-value-analysis">boundary values</Term> of dates: **the last day of each month** (28, 29, 30, 31), **February 29 in a leap year** (2028) and **February 28 in a normal year** (2027), **December 31 → January 1**, **DST change days**, and **times around midnight**. The playground marks them with colored dots.
</Callout>

You can't test all 365 days. Split them into <Term id="equivalence-partitioning">partitions</Term> (past, today, near future, far future, beyond the booking window) and test one date from each, plus the boundaries between them: yesterday, today, the last bookable day, and the day after it.

## Accessibility

Calendars are hard to use without sight or a mouse. Check these:

- **Every day has a full name.** A <Term id="screen-reader" /> should say "Tuesday, 20 October 2026", not just "20". Today and the selected state must be announced too ("today", "selected").
- **Keyboard (web):** a calendar grid should support the arrow keys (day and week), Home/End (start and end of the week) and Page Up/Page Down (month). Try it in the gallery and playground above.
- **Typing must always be possible.** Some users can't operate wheels or tiny day cells. WCAG 2.2 asks for a single-pointer alternative to dragging movements (2.5.7), and a text input is the easiest alternative of all. Don't make a text field read-only just because there is a calendar button.
- **Show the format** in a visible label or hint (`DD/MM/YYYY`), not only as a <Term id="placeholder" /> that disappears while typing.
- **Errors in words:** "April has only 30 days" beats a red border alone (don't rely on color).
- **Disabled days** must look disabled for more than color (for example a strikethrough) and be announced as unavailable.
- **Modal pickers** must move focus into the dialog, keep it there (<Term id="focus-trap" />), and return it to the field when closed.
- **<Term id="touch-target">Touch targets</Term>:** day cells on small phones are often below 44×44 pt (iOS) / 48×48 dp (Android). Check them.
- **Autofill:** browsers and password managers should be able to fill birth date fields.
  <DevNote>Birth date fields should use `autocomplete="bday"` (or `bday-day`, `bday-month`, `bday-year`).</DevNote>

<KeyTakeaways>

- Date pickers come as **calendars, wheels, dials, typed fields and range pickers**. Each fits a different job, and a calendar for a birth date is overkill.
- The same digits mean different dates in different **locales** (03/04). Always show the format and test at least en-US and one European locale.
- A **calendar date is not a timestamp**. Change the device time zone and use times near midnight to catch dates that shift by a day.
- Always test the **boundaries**: month ends, Feb 29 in leap and non-leap years, Dec 31 → Jan 1, DST change days, 12:00 AM/PM.
- For ranges, check **order** (end after start) and **counts** (nights = difference, not difference + 1).
- Test **both typing and picking**, and check the value that actually reaches the server.

</KeyTakeaways>
